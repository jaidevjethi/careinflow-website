A patient deciding between two clinics reads your reviews before they read
your website. Not all of them. The score, how many there are, the most
recent few, and, more than anything, how you answered the unhappy ones.

Your reputation is built in the clinic: in how long people wait, how the
phone is answered and how clearly things are explained. Online is where it
is read, by strangers, at night, next to your competitors. This guide
covers how to look after that reading without breaking Google's rules or
your patients' privacy.

## What do patients check in a clinic's reviews?

Watch someone compare two practices and the order is consistent:

1. **The rating, and how many reviews it rests on.** A 4.8 from twelve
   reviews and a 4.4 from three hundred tell a patient different things.
2. **How recent they are.** A profile whose last review is from last year
   looks closed, or at least unloved.
3. **What people complain about.** Patients forgive one bad day. They
   notice a pattern: long waits, unclear bills, a rude phone call.
4. **Whether the clinic replies, and how.** A calm reply to a hard review
   often reassures more than five good ones.
5. **Whether the details agree.** Timings, phone number and address that
   differ between Google and your website make a patient wonder which is
   wrong.

## How can a clinic get more Google reviews?

Ask every patient, the same way, every time. Many will leave a
review if you make it easy and ask while they are still at the counter.
Few will think of it on their own.

Three ways that work in a clinic:

- **A link or QR code at reception.** Google gives every Business Profile
  a short link and a QR code that open the review form directly. Print the
  code and keep it by the counter.
- **A WhatsApp message after the visit,** with the same link, sent the
  same day.
- **One sentence from the receptionist** at checkout, to every patient:
  "A Google review of your visit helps other patients know what to
  expect."

## What does Google not allow?

These are not etiquette. Google's own policies prohibit them, and it
removes reviews that break them:

- **Incentives.** No discount, free consultation, gift or payment in
  return for a review. Google calls this fake engagement.
- **Asking only the happy ones.** Selectively asking for positive reviews,
  or discouraging negative ones, counts as rating manipulation.
- **Reviews from people connected to the clinic.** Staff, former staff,
  family and business partners have a conflict of interest, and their
  reviews are not allowed.
- **Writing or buying reviews.** Beyond being removed, a cluster of
  similar new reviews is visible to anyone who reads them, including the
  patients you were trying to impress.

## How should a doctor reply to a negative review?

The reply is written for the next patient reading it, not for the person
who wrote the review. Four rules:

1. **Reply calmly, within a day or two.** A fast, measured answer reads as
   a clinic that is paying attention.
2. **Never confirm the reviewer was your patient,** and never mention a
   diagnosis, a treatment or anything from their visit. Confidentiality
   does not stop at the clinic door, and a public reply is public forever.
3. **Acknowledge the concern in general terms and move it offline.** Offer
   a phone number and a name to ask for.
4. **Fix the cause if the complaint is fair.** If three reviews mention
   waiting time, the answer is in the appointment book, not the reply box.

A reply that keeps all four rules can be as short as this:

> Thank you for taking the time to write this. We are sorry your visit
> fell short of what you expected, and we would like to understand what
> happened. Please call the clinic on [number] and ask for [name].

Reply to good reviews too: briefly, in your own words each time, and
without repeating anything personal the patient mentioned.

## What if a review is fake or abusive?

Flag it from your Business Profile. Google checks flagged reviews against
its policies and removes those that break them: spam, fake reviews,
conflicts of interest, abusive or off-topic content, and personal
information posted without consent. A review that is simply critical will
stay, however unfair it feels.

While Google decides, reply once, calmly, in the same way as any other
critical review. Do not argue in public. A patient reading a heated
exchange remembers the doctor's tone, not who was right.

## Is reputation only about reviews?

No. Reviews are the loudest signal, but patients build their impression
from everything they find:

- **The same details everywhere.** Name, address, phone and timings
  identical on your website, your Google listing and any directory that
  lists your clinic, such as Practo or Justdial.
- **A website that answers what reviews raise.** If people ask about cost,
  waiting time or what a procedure involves, a clear page on each answers
  them before they need to ask.
  [What a clinic website actually needs](/resources/what-a-clinic-website-actually-needs/)
  covers which pages those are.
- **Real photographs** of your clinic, your team and your equipment,
  rather than stock pictures a patient has seen on three other sites.
- **Your own voice.** Short videos in which you explain things clearly let
  patients judge you directly, not only through other people's reviews.
  [Social media for doctors](/resources/social-media-for-doctors/) covers
  how to make them.

## How often should a clinic check its reviews?

Weekly at the least, with replies within a day or two, and once a month a
look at the themes: what keeps coming up, good and bad. That monthly look
is where reviews become useful to the practice rather than only to its
reputation.
