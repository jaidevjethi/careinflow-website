Most clinic websites are built from the clinic's point of view: a welcome
message, a mission statement, a photo of the building. Patients, meanwhile,
arrive with one worried question and a thumb hovering over the back
button.

A website works when it is organized around the patient's question. Here
is the short list of what that requires.

## Why every treatment needs its own page

This is the single highest-value structure decision. A patient searching
"root canal cost Mehsana" wants a page about root canals — what happens,
how long it takes, what affects the cost, what recovery feels like. One
generic "Our Services" list cannot rank for that search or answer that
patient.

Write each page in the words patients use: symptom and cost, not clinical
terminology. "Tooth pain" and "crooked teeth" outperform "endodontics" and
"malocclusion" because that is what people type.

## Should a clinic publish its prices?

Clinics avoid publishing prices, usually for defensible reasons. But total
silence pushes patients to whoever says anything at all. There is a middle
path: publish ranges, starting points, or a plain explanation of what
affects the cost. "A single implant starts from X; the final cost depends
on…" respects both the patient and the clinical reality.

## Why real photos beat stock images

The building, the rooms, the equipment, the team — real photos are trust
evidence. Stock photography of models in white coats actively hurts:
patients recognize it, and it signals that the real clinic is not worth
showing.

## Make contacting you effortless

Every page should end with an obvious, low-friction action. In Gujarat
that is usually WhatsApp: a button that opens a pre-written message, so
the patient only presses send. Tap-to-call and tap-for-directions cover
the rest. If contacting you requires copying a number or filling a
five-field form, you are losing the hesitant majority.

## How fast should your website be?

Your patients are not on your office Wi-Fi. They are on mid-range Android
phones on mobile data, where a heavy website takes six seconds to appear
and gets abandoned in three. Speed is a design decision — few scripts,
optimized images, nothing autoplaying. We cover why this matters for
search as well in [website speed and patient trust](/resources/website-speed-and-patient-trust).

## Features your clinic website does not need

In our experience these add cost and nothing else: image sliders (nobody
waits for slide three), background music, animated counters, chatbots that
know less than the receptionist, and PDF brochures that no phone opens
happily. If a feature does not answer a patient question or shorten the
path to contact, it is decoration.

## A two-minute test for your own website

Open your website and pretend to be a patient with a specific problem —
say, a chipped front tooth. Count the taps until you find what the clinic
would do about it and what it might cost, then count the taps to actually
make contact. More than a couple of each, and the site is working against
you. This test is a large part of how we structure every
[clinic website we build](/services/healthcare-websites).
