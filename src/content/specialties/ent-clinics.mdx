## Why is ENT three businesses in one practice?

Because the patients have nothing in common except which doctor they end up seeing.

A parent whose two-year-old has had four ear infections since Diwali. An adult who has not breathed properly through one nostril in two years and has stopped mentioning it. A family sitting down to work out whether a hearing aid for their father is worth what it costs.

Three different fears, three different vocabularies, three different speeds of decision, and three different people doing the searching. The toddler is searched for by a parent. The sinus patient searches for themselves, eventually. The hearing aid is very often searched by an adult child.

One homepage trying to greet all three greets none of them. This is the specialty where the split matters most, and where almost every practice website is one undifferentiated list of conditions.

## What does the paediatric side need?

Its own section, written for a parent rather than for a patient.

Recurring ear infections, tonsils and adenoids are among the most-searched problems in ENT, and the parent doing the searching has one question above all others: does my child really need an operation.

The page that earns that family answers it honestly, including the cases where the answer is wait and see, with a plain account of what waiting involves and what would change the recommendation. A parent told the truth about their child comes back when the time arrives, and tells other parents in the meantime.

## Why do hearing aids belong on their own pages?

Because that is retail with a clinical component, and it behaves like retail.

Hearing aid searches are price-led and comparison-heavy. People shop across three or four providers, compare models, and take weeks. It is much closer to buying spectacles than to booking a consultation, and it frequently involves someone other than the wearer paying and deciding.

Treating it as one line on a Services page loses all of that. It deserves its own pages, its own Google Business Profile category, and pricing written the way a shopper needs it: what the ranges actually are, what changes the figure, what the trial and fitting process involves, and what happens afterwards when it needs adjusting.

Practices that write it honestly do well here, because most of the competition is either silent about price or quoting a starting figure nobody pays.

## What is the search nobody is writing for?

Snoring.

People search snoring years before they search sleep apnoea, because snoring is what their family complains about and apnoea is a word they have not met. Almost every ENT site jumps straight to the clinical term, which means the page is written for a patient who has already been diagnosed.

A page that begins where the patient begins, and explains plainly when snoring stops being a domestic nuisance and becomes a medical problem worth investigating, catches an audience the rest of the market is ignoring. It also brings in a patient who is often otherwise healthy and easy to treat.

## How should the practice be listed on Google?

With more than one category, because it is genuinely more than one thing.

An ENT practice that also fits hearing aids is eligible for categories covering both, and choosing only the clinical one quietly removes the practice from every hearing aid search in the district. If there is an audiologist, that is a further category again.

Choosing them is part of [Google Business Profile management](/services/google-business-profile/), and it is the fastest win available to an ENT practice: a hearing aid category added on a Tuesday can change what the district sees by the weekend.
