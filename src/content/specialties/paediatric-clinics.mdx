## Who is actually searching, and when?

Not the patient. Not ever.

A child cannot describe a symptom, cannot search, and cannot decide. The person on your website is a parent, usually a mother, frequently at ten at night or four in the morning, watching a fever that will not come down and trying to work out whether this is serious.

Every other specialty on this site writes for the person with the problem. Paediatrics is the one where the reader is watching the problem in someone else, with far less information and considerably more fear.

That changes everything about the page. The tone has to be calm. The most important information has to be at the top, not after three paragraphs about the clinic. And the site has to load on a phone, on mobile data, in a dark room, quickly.

## What is the one page a paediatric clinic must have?

When to bring your child in.

Written properly, it is the most valuable page in the specialty. Clear signs that mean come now. Clear signs that mean watch and call in the morning. Plain language about fever thresholds by age, breathing, fluids, and the things that genuinely cannot wait.

It reaches parents at the exact moment they are searching, which no other page does. It reduces calls that did not need to happen. And it makes the calls that should happen arrive earlier, which is better medicine as well as better business.

It needs your clinical sign-off, and it needs to say plainly that it is guidance rather than a diagnosis. We write it, you approve it, and nothing goes live that you have not agreed to.

## Why do opening hours matter more here than anywhere else?

Because paediatric searching is disproportionately out of hours, and the consequence of getting hours wrong is immediate.

A parent who drives to a closed clinic with a sick child at eight in the evening does not usually try you again. They go to whoever was open, and that practice keeps the family for years, because a paediatrician is chosen once and then kept.

So your published hours have to be exactly right, including festival and holiday closures. If you run an evening session, take walk-ins, or answer calls after hours, both the website and the Google listing have to say so, and they have to agree with each other. This is the most common thing we find wrong on a paediatric listing, and it is the cheapest to fix.

## What keeps parents coming back to the site?

The vaccination schedule.

It is the most reliable search traffic in the specialty, because the same parent looks it up repeatedly over several years. Which one is due at nine months. What to do about a dose missed during a move. Whether the optional ones are worth it. What happens at the visit, and whether the child will be unwell afterwards.

A schedule page written plainly is searched all year rather than seasonally, and it introduces your clinic to parents who have not chosen a paediatrician yet. Very few practices here publish one properly.

## How do parents choose a paediatrician?

Differently from every other specialty, and mostly on trust rather than on price.

Paediatrics is chosen once and kept for a decade, so the decision is weighted toward reassurance: who else brings their children here, whether the doctor explains things patiently, whether the clinic is calm, whether appointments run to time with a distressed toddler in the room. Price barely features.

What that means for the website is that warmth is not decoration here, it is the product. The photographs should be of your actual clinic, the writing should sound like a person, and the enquiry should reach someone quickly, because a worried parent will not fill in a form.
